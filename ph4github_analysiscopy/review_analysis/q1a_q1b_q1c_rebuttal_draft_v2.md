## Revised strategy

### 1a should be stronger, but precise

The strongest defensible distinction is not "we use Bayesian/RL for pH control," because the reviewer correctly points out that this broader space already exists. The real technical highlight supported by the local code is the staged hybrid workflow:

1. an online chemistry-grounded Bayesian controller that updates latent acid-base parameters during titration,
2. distillation of that controller into a fast neural policy using Bayesian-generated expert trajectories,
3. RL refinement of the distilled policy rather than RL-from-scratch,
4. deployment of different stages on real experimental tasks.

This is stronger than a defensive novelty claim because it identifies the functional role of each stage:

- Bayesian stage: mechanistic teacher and uncertainty-aware decision engine.
- Imitation stage: computational compression of the teacher into a deployable policy.
- RL stage: policy refinement on top of the teacher-derived prior.

The local code supports this directly:

- Bayesian teacher trajectories are generated by repeatedly calling the Bayesian controller (`review_outputs/logs/main_code3_cell_3.py`).
- The imitation model is trained on those Bayesian-generated labels, so it is teacher distillation rather than generic simulation supervision.
- The RL stage reuses the same action head and fine-tunes the distilled policy.

The stage-wise benchmark metrics also support the practical value of the decomposition:

- Bayesian success rate: 90.30%
- Imitation success rate: 93.87%
- Reinforcement success rate: 94.30%

### 1b should be quantitative and conservative

I reran a 3000-task interpretability audit on the local benchmark conditions and recorded posterior pKa means, posterior standard deviations, and slot-usage diagnostics across titration steps.

Main findings:

- Overall matched posterior pKa MAE was 0.878 initially, 0.884 by `min(step, 8)`, and 0.885 at the final step.
- The fraction of tasks for which all true pKa values were recovered within `+-1.0` pH units was 47.10% by `min(step, 8)` and 47.13% at the final step.
- The fraction of tasks for which all true pKa values were recovered within `+-0.5` pH units was 24.80% by `min(step, 8)` and 24.90% at the final step.
- Mean posterior pKa standard deviation changed only modestly, from 0.200 initially to 0.190 by `min(step, 8)` and 0.192 at the final step.

By acid class:

- Monoprotic: all true pKa values within `+-1.0` pH units for 82.39% of tasks by `min(step, 8)`.
- Diprotic: 51.41%.
- Triprotic: 6.56%.

These numbers imply:

- The current Bayesian layer does expose interpretable intermediate latent quantities.
- However, those quantities do not support a strong claim of reliable chemical identification, especially for more complex systems.
- Any statement that approximately eight titration measurements are generally sufficient to infer the true number of species and their pKa values would be too strong for the current implementation.

There is an even stronger architectural reason to narrow the species-count claim:

- The current Bayesian controller always keeps three latent buffer slots.
- It does not explicitly maintain a posterior over the number of species.
- The extra-slot diagnostic confirms that unused slots are not cleanly suppressed:
  - Monoprotic tasks: mean unused-slot mole fraction 0.668 at the final step.
  - Diprotic tasks: mean unused-slot mole fraction 0.333 at the final step.

So the right conclusion for 1b is not "the Bayesian module identifies species count," but rather:

- it provides a structured latent chemical representation,
- it exposes posterior means and uncertainties step by step,
- but its current local evidence supports only limited pKa identifiability and does not support direct species-count identification.

### 1c should emphasize why the Bayesian stage adds value

It is important to answer this without sounding evasive. The most effective reply is:

- A direct neural network trained on simulated data still needs target actions.
- In the present workflow, those target actions are not arbitrary simulator outputs; they are produced by a chemistry-constrained, uncertainty-aware Bayesian controller.
- Therefore, the Bayesian stage is not merely a way of "making more simulated data." It defines the structure and quality of the supervision.

The specific advantages supported by local evidence are:

1. Structured supervision rather than raw simulation labels.
   The Bayesian teacher produces actions using charge-balance/equilibrium calculations plus posterior uncertainty, so the imitation model learns a chemically informed policy instead of regressing against an ad hoc label rule.

2. Uncertainty-aware action generation.
   The teacher modulates aggressiveness using posterior pKa uncertainty, which a plain simulation-only label generator would not automatically provide.

3. Compression of a mechanistic controller into a fast deployable network.
   The local timing benchmark shows:
   - Bayesian update + select: 53.57 ms mean latency per decision
   - Imitation forward pass: 0.166 ms
   - RL forward pass: 0.211 ms
   So the learned policy keeps the teacher's logic while making runtime decision cost negligible relative to the 20 s physical experimental cycle.

4. Better initialization for RL.
   RL is not trained from scratch in this workflow; it starts from a distilled policy already shaped by the Bayesian teacher.

What we should still state explicitly:

- The current local repo does not include a separate "direct supervised NN without Bayesian teacher" baseline.
- Therefore, we should frame these as architectural/principled advantages of the Bayesian stage, not as an already completed empirical head-to-head victory over every direct-supervised alternative.

That balance keeps the answer strong without overclaiming.

## Draft rebuttal text

### Reviewer 1a

We thank the reviewer for this important comment. We agree that the distinguishing point of the present work should not be framed simply as the use of Bayesian or reinforcement-learning ideas in pH control, because related controller families already exist. We have therefore revised the manuscript to clarify that the main contribution is the staged integration of three roles within a single titration workflow: an online chemistry-grounded Bayesian controller, distillation of that controller into a fast neural policy, and subsequent RL refinement of the distilled policy. In our implementation, the Bayesian module is not only a controller but also the source of expert trajectories for imitation learning, while the RL stage refines a policy that is already informed by the Bayesian teacher rather than being trained from scratch. We believe this teacher-distillation-refinement pipeline, together with validation on real titration tasks, is the more accurate statement of what is distinctive about the work, and we have revised the manuscript accordingly.

### Reviewer 1b

We appreciate the reviewer's request for a more concrete justification of interpretability, and we agree that this should be answered quantitatively rather than rhetorically. We therefore performed a 3000-task analysis on the local simulated benchmark conditions and tracked the Bayesian posterior quantities step by step. Using an optimistic post hoc matching between the latent posterior pKa slots and the true pKa values, the mean matched pKa MAE was 0.878 at initialization, 0.884 after up to 8 titration steps, and 0.885 at the final step. Likewise, the fraction of tasks for which all true pKa values were recovered within +-1.0 pH units was 47.10% after up to 8 steps and 47.13% at the final step. Performance depended strongly on system complexity: after up to 8 steps, this fraction was 82.39% for monoprotic systems, 51.41% for diprotic systems, and only 6.56% for triprotic systems. We also found only modest uncertainty contraction, with the mean posterior pKa standard deviation changing from 0.200 initially to 0.190 after up to 8 steps.

These results support a more precise and narrower interpretation of what the Bayesian layer provides in practice. It does expose explicit posterior means and posterior uncertainties, so the internal chemical state is visible rather than purely black-box. However, the present implementation does not support a strong claim that roughly eight titration measurements are generally sufficient to infer the exact number of species and their pKa values. This is also consistent with the model structure itself: the current Bayesian controller always maintains three latent buffer slots and does not explicitly maintain a posterior over species count. We have therefore revised the manuscript to describe the Bayesian layer as a structured and uncertainty-aware latent chemical representation, while removing any language that could be read as claiming reliable species-count identification from a small number of titration steps.

### Reviewer 1c

We thank the reviewer for raising this point, because it helps clarify the actual role of the Bayesian stage in our workflow. In the present implementation, the imitation-learning model is trained on trajectories generated by the Bayesian controller, so the comparison is not between "Bayesian policy" and an arbitrary neural network trained on generic simulated data. Rather, the Bayesian stage defines the supervision itself: it produces actions through chemistry-constrained equilibrium calculations together with posterior uncertainty updates, and those actions become the expert labels used for policy distillation. In this sense, the advantage of the Bayesian stage is not simply that it generates more simulated data, but that it generates structured, uncertainty-aware, chemically informed supervision.

This staged design also provides a practical deployment benefit. In our local timing benchmark, one Bayesian update-plus-action-selection cycle required about 53.57 ms on average, whereas the distilled imitation policy and the RL-refined policy required only about 0.166 ms and 0.211 ms, respectively. Thus, the Bayesian stage provides the mechanistic teacher, while the learned policy compresses that logic into a much cheaper runtime controller. The RL stage then refines a policy that already starts from this chemistry-informed prior, rather than searching from scratch. We have revised the manuscript to emphasize these roles more clearly. At the same time, we now state explicitly that the current local codebase does not yet include a separate direct-supervised neural-network baseline trained without the Bayesian teacher, so our claim is framed as a principled advantage of the workflow rather than a completed empirical head-to-head comparison against every direct-simulation alternative.

## Recommended manuscript revision language

For the introduction / method-positioning section:

> Rather than positioning this study as the first use of Bayesian or reinforcement-learning ideas for pH control, we position it as a staged hybrid workflow in which an online chemistry-informed Bayesian controller is first used as an uncertainty-aware decision engine, then distilled into a fast neural policy, and finally refined by reinforcement learning.

For the interpretability claim:

> In this work, interpretability refers to the fact that the Bayesian layer maintains explicit intermediate latent quantities, including inferred acidity-related parameters and their posterior uncertainty, which directly modulate the recommended dosing action. We do not use this term to imply guaranteed recovery of the exact number of chemical species or their true pKa values from a small number of titration steps.

For the Bayesian-vs-direct-NN motivation:

> The value of the Bayesian stage in the present workflow is that it provides chemically structured, uncertainty-aware supervision for policy distillation and a principled initialization for subsequent RL refinement, while the distilled neural policy reduces deployment-time decision cost.
